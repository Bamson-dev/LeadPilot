### Full rendered body

A few years ago, the honest answer to someone who said they wanted to build software without coding was uncomfortable: learn to code, or find serious money and hire.

That answer is no longer complete, and I would rather explain the change carefully than exaggerate it.

What genuinely changed is access to execution. Describing what a screen should do, what should happen when a button is pressed, what data should be stored and how errors should be handled can now produce working output far faster than before. A person with clear thinking and patience can get to a testable version without years of formal training.

What did not change is everything on the business side. AI cannot tell you whether a clinic actually cares about your feature. It cannot confirm that the person you are speaking to controls a budget. It cannot decide your price, hold a customer conversation for you, or notice that your buyer has already tried three similar tools and abandoned them.

This is why speed can be dangerous. Building faster in the wrong direction simply gets you to a polished wrong answer sooner. A wrong product built in one week is still a wrong product; you have only lost less time discovering it.

The useful way to think about it is direction and execution. AI has quietly become very good at execution. Direction is still yours. Choosing the problem, judging the evidence, deciding what to leave out, talking to the person who pays, and making the trade-offs when something has to give.

There is a second thing that did not change: verification. AI will produce something confidently even when it has misunderstood you. If you do not test what it built, you will ship your own misunderstanding. That habit of checking, comparing the result with what you actually asked for, is not optional.

So the realistic picture is this. The technical barrier that used to stop non-technical people has come down a long way. The commercial standard has not moved at all. Both of those statements are true at the same time, and anybody telling you only the first half is selling you a fantasy.

Here is a useful test for the week. Take a task you already understand and try to describe it precisely enough that a very literal assistant could do it correctly. Where you struggle to be precise is where your thinking is still fuzzy, and fuzzy thinking is what breaks builds.

Ideas are not scarce. Observation is. Most people have simply never been taught how to look, so they sit and wait for something to arrive fully formed.

Let me give you three methods that work whether or not you feel creative today.

The first is shadowing. Ask one business owner to let you watch a task they repeat weekly, from the moment it starts to the moment it is finished. Do not offer suggestions. Just record every handoff, every place data is retyped, every wait, every reminder, every decision made with missing information. A single hour of this usually produces more material than a month of thinking.

The second is auditing your own week. Look at where your work gets repetitive: the same message you retype, the same figures you copy between apps, the same follow-up you forget until it is late. You already have privileged access to this one, and familiarity is the only reason it feels uninteresting.

The third is listening for workaround language. People rarely announce problems. They describe compromises. Listen for phrases like we just manage it, I usually copy it into Excel, we keep it in a book, somebody has to remember, we call to confirm. Every one of those sentences marks a place where the current method is failing quietly.

That third method is the one I rely on most, because a workaround is proof. It means the pain was real enough that somebody invented an unpaid solution for it. Where there is a workaround, there is already effort being spent.

Now a warning, because collecting is the easy half. Do not fall in love with the first thing you find. A problem being real does not mean it is worth building for. You still have to ask how often it happens, what it costs when it goes wrong, who would authorise paying for a fix, and why the existing workaround has survived this long.

Practical assignment for this week: fill one page with observations, not ideas. Aim for ten entries. Every time I talk about building software, somebody replies that the market is saturated and everything has already been built.

It is a reasonable observation. It is also usually a conclusion drawn from the wrong evidence.

Consider what a crowded category actually tells you. It tells you money is being spent there. Nobody builds twenty competing tools for a problem nobody has. A completely empty category is often empty for a reason, and that reason is usually that people will not pay.

But I will not turn that into a comfortable slogan. Competition does not guarantee you anything. Plenty of people enter crowded markets and get nowhere, because they arrive with a slightly worse copy of an existing tool and no reason for anybody to switch.

The useful distinction is between a category and a workflow. Accounting software as a category is completely served. Hope sounds like this: people will definitely use this, everybody has this problem, my friends said it is a great idea.

Evidence sounds completely different, and once you can hear the difference you stop wasting months on polite encouragement.

Weak signals are the ones that cost the other person nothing. Compliments. Thumbs up. I would use that. Send me the link when it is ready. All of these feel wonderful and predict almost nothing, because agreeing with you is free and most people would rather be pleasant than accurate.

Strong signals cost something. Somebody already paying for a worse solution. Somebody willing to open their spreadsheet and show you how the work is really done. Somebody giving you forty minutes on a working day. Somebody introducing you to the person who controls the budget. Somebody agreeing to test a rough version with real data. Notice the pattern. Time, access, data, money, and introductions are scarce. When people spend them on you, they are telling you the pain is real. Words are not scarce.

The strongest signal of all is that they are already spending money or labour on the problem. If a business pays somebody to do a task manually every week, the budget already exists. You are not asking them to create a new line of spending, only to move it.

There is a trap on the other side too. A single enthusiastic person is not a market. One buyer with an unusual situation can pull you into building something that fits them alone. Look for the same pain described in similar words by several people who do not know each other.

So how do you gather this without a product? You ask about the past instead of the future. Do not ask whether they would use a tool. Ask what they did last time the problem occurred, what it cost them, what they tried, and why they stopped. Past behaviour is evidence. Future intentions are imagination with good manners.

Try three of those conversations this week. Twenty minutes each, no pitching. If you leave with somebody willing to show you their process or discuss what a fix would be worth, you have learned more than a month of planning would have taught you.

None of this removes risk. People can show every good signal and still not buy when the moment comes. The point is not certainty. It is making your next decision with better information than a compliment.

Think back over the last two years and count the opportunities you got genuinely excited about. Forex. Dropshipping. Crypto. Affiliate marketing. Print on demand. Something a friend was doing that looked like it was working.

I am not mocking any of that. I have been enthusiastic about things that went nowhere too. What I want you to notice is the pattern rather than the individual choices.

Each time, the sequence was similar. Real excitement. A few weeks of learning. Some tutorials, maybe a group. Then difficulty arrives, results are slower than promised, and a newer opportunity appears that has not disappointed you yet. You move. The cycle restarts.

The cost of that cycle is not mainly money, and I am not going to invent a figure for what you have lost. The real cost is that you keep restarting the hardest part.

In any skill, the early weeks are the most expensive. You are slow, confused, and producing bad work. The payoff comes later, when the confusion thins out and your judgment starts to be worth something. Hopping means paying the expensive part repeatedly and collecting the payoff never.

There is a second cost that is harder to see. Every abandoned attempt teaches you something about yourself, and the lesson is usually the wrong one. After enough cycles, people conclude they are not disciplined, or that nothing works in this country, or that success requires connections they do not have. The actual problem was that nothing was ever carried to a finish.

Doing nothing has the same shape, just quieter. Nothing is lost on paper. But a year passes and the skill you wanted still does not exist, and the version of you who could have been two years into practising it is gone.

So the question I would ask before you choose anything else, including my work, is this: are you willing to stay with one thing long enough to get past the ugly part? Not forever. Long enough to complete one full cycle and see a real result, good or bad.

Here is a way to test yourself honestly. Write down what completing one cycle would look like in concrete terms. For software, it might be: one investigated problem, one written blueprint, one working version, one real user, one conversation about payment.
